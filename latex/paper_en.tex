\begin{abstract}
%----------------------------------------------------------------------
We construct the full phase diagram of an AI-driven labor market automation
model in the two-dimensional control-parameter space spanned by the Basic
Digital Payment (BDP) transfer rate and the CES elasticity of substitution
$\sigma$ between labor and capital. Using a stock-flow consistent agent-based
model (SFC-ABM) with 10{,}000 firms across six GUS-calibrated sectors, we
identify a critical line $\mathrm{BDP}_c(\sigma)$ that separates a low-adoption
phase from a high-adoption (cascade) phase, with a bimodal coexistence region
at intermediate values. We demonstrate that this phase boundary is
topologically universal: Watts--Strogatz, Erd\H{o}s--R\'{e}nyi, Barab\'{a}si--Albert,
and square-lattice network substrates yield statistically indistinguishable
critical points and bifurcation envelopes, differing only in variance.
Finite-size scaling analysis at system sizes $N \in \{1000, 5000, 10000,
50000\}$ reveals a susceptibility peak that diverges as $\chi_{\max} \sim
N^{\gamma/\bar{\nu}}$, with a critical BDP that converges as $\mathrm{BDP}_c(N) -
\mathrm{BDP}_c(\infty) \sim N^{-1/\bar{\nu}}$. The extracted exponents
$\beta$, $\gamma$, and $\bar{\nu}$ are compared against mean-field,
two-dimensional Ising, and percolation universality classes. Decision-rule
perturbation experiments confirm that the critical point survives
modifications to the demographic weighting and risk-aversion parameters,
establishing the phase transition as a robust emergent property of the
coupled fiscal--technological dynamics rather than a modeling artifact.

\medskip\noindent
\textbf{Keywords:} phase transition, agent-based model, stock-flow consistency,
AI automation, universality, finite-size scaling, labor market

\medskip\noindent
\textbf{JEL Codes:} C63, E17, J23, O33
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}
%======================================================================

The study of phase transitions has been among the most productive unifying
frameworks in the natural sciences. Systems as diverse as ferromagnets, binary
alloys, percolation lattices, and epidemic networks all exhibit qualitatively
similar behavior near critical points: order parameters vanish continuously or
discontinuously, fluctuations diverge, and the microscopic details of the
interaction become irrelevant to the macroscopic phenomenology
\parencite{Stanley1971}. The renormalization group provides the theoretical
scaffolding for this universality, showing that critical exponents depend only
on dimensionality and the symmetry of the order parameter, not on lattice
structure or coupling constants \parencite{Fisher1998}.

Whether analogous universality holds in socioeconomic systems remains an open
and consequential question. Agent-based models (ABMs) in economics routinely
produce sharp transitions---bank runs, technology cascades, opinion
flips---but these are rarely subjected to the finite-size scaling (FSS)
analysis that would distinguish a genuine phase transition from a finite-size
crossover \parencite{Castellano2009}. The stakes are practical: if a labor
market automation threshold is a true critical point, then policy interventions
exhibit divergent sensitivity near it; if it is merely a smooth crossover
inflated by small system size, the urgency is qualitatively different.

In a companion paper, \textcite{Maciaszek2025a} documented a bimodal
distribution of AI adoption rates at a Basic Digital Payment (BDP) level of
2{,}000~PLN in a stock-flow consistent agent-based model (SFC-ABM) of the
Polish labor market. The Hartigan dip test rejected unimodality at $p =
1.7 \times 10^{-5}$, and Bayesian information criteria selected a
three-component Gaussian mixture. A subsequent study \parencite{Maciaszek2025b}
showed that the monetary regime (PLN float vs.\ EUR with Stability and Growth
Pact constraints) shifts the critical BDP by a factor of two, while
\textcite{Maciaszek2025c} demonstrated that even order-of-magnitude changes in
the CES elasticity of substitution $\sigma$ alter the adoption rate by only
$\sim$1.5~percentage points---far less than the 6~pp shift induced by the
monetary regime change.

These findings raise four questions that the present paper addresses:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Phase boundary geometry.} What is the shape of the critical
    line $\mathrm{BDP}_c(\sigma)$ in the two-dimensional control-parameter
    space, and does the bimodal region form a closed wedge or extend to
    arbitrarily large $\sigma$?

    \item \textbf{Topology universality.} Does the critical point survive when
    the Watts--Strogatz firm interaction network is replaced by
    Erd\H{o}s--R\'{e}nyi, Barab\'{a}si--Albert, or regular lattice topologies?

    \item \textbf{Universality class.} What are the critical exponents $\beta$,
    $\gamma$, and $\bar{\nu}$, and do they correspond to a known universality
    class (mean-field, Ising, percolation)?

    \item \textbf{Finite-size scaling.} Does FSS analysis at system sizes
    $N \in \{1{,}000,\; 5{,}000,\; 10{,}000,\; 50{,}000\}$ confirm a
    divergent susceptibility, ruling out finite-size artifacts?
\end{enumerate}

The paper proceeds as follows. Section~\ref{sec:model} recaps the SFC-ABM
architecture and introduces the new topology variants and variable system
sizes. Section~\ref{sec:method} describes the four simulation campaigns and the
statistical methodology for phase boundary identification, exponent extraction,
and FSS. Section~\ref{sec:results} presents results.
Section~\ref{sec:discussion} interprets the findings in the context of
universality theory and policy design. Section~\ref{sec:conclusion} concludes.

\section{Model Architecture}
\label{sec:model}
%======================================================================

\subsection{SFC-ABM overview}

The model extends the stock-flow consistent agent-based framework described in
\textcite{Maciaszek2025a} and \textcite{Maciaszek2025b}. The economy consists
of $N = 10{,}000$ firms distributed across six sectors calibrated to the Polish
Central Statistical Office (GUS) 2024 data, a consolidated household sector, a
commercial banking sector, a central bank operating a Taylor-rule interest rate
policy, and a government that finances a Basic Digital Payment (BDP) transfer.
All financial flows satisfy the quadruple-entry accounting identities of the
SFC tradition \parencite{Godley2007}, ensuring that every monetary unit created
has a counterpart liability.

Each firm occupies one of four technology states---Traditional, Hybrid,
Automated, or Bankrupt---and transitions between them according to a stochastic
decision rule that depends on the firm's profitability, the adoption rate in
its network neighborhood, and sector-specific parameters (the CES elasticity of
substitution $\sigma_s$, digital readiness $d_s$, and hybrid labor retention
rate $h_s$). The simulation runs for 120~months with an exogenous AI
availability shock at month~30, after which firms may begin investing in
automation. The BDP transfer, denominated in PLN, is paid monthly to all
households beginning at the shock date and financed by government borrowing
subject to the monetary regime's fiscal constraints.

For a complete specification of the decision rule, sectoral parameters, balance
sheet identities, and the Taylor-rule monetary policy, we refer the reader to
\textcite{Maciaszek2025a} (Appendix~A) and \textcite{Maciaszek2025b}
(Section~3). Here we describe only the extensions introduced for the present
study.

\subsection{Network topology variants}

The baseline model employs a Watts--Strogatz (WS) small-world network with
mean degree $k = 6$ and rewiring probability $p = 0.10$
\parencite{Watts1998}. To test whether the phase transition depends on network
structure, we implement three additional topologies, each constructed to
preserve the mean degree $\langle k \rangle = 6$ for comparability:

\begin{table}[htbp]
\centering
\caption{Network topology properties. All topologies are constructed with
$N = 10{,}000$ nodes and mean degree $\langle k \rangle = 6$.}
\label{tab:topologies}
\begin{tabular}{lcccc}
\toprule
Topology & Clustering $C$ & Char.\ path $L$ & Degree dist.\ & Reference \\
\midrule
Watts--Strogatz & \todo{C\_WS} & \todo{L\_WS} & narrow & \textcite{Watts1998} \\
Erd\H{o}s--R\'{e}nyi & \todo{C\_ER} & \todo{L\_ER} & Poisson & \textcite{Erdos1959} \\
Barab\'{a}si--Albert & \todo{C\_BA} & \todo{L\_BA} & power-law & \textcite{Barabasi1999} \\
Square lattice & \todo{C\_LAT} & \todo{L\_LAT} & $\delta(k{-}4)$ & --- \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{table}

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Erd\H{o}s--R\'{e}nyi (ER).} Each pair of nodes is connected
    independently with probability $p = k / (N-1)$, yielding a Poisson degree
    distribution with mean~$k$ \parencite{Erdos1959}. The resulting graph has
    low clustering and short path lengths.

    \item \textbf{Barab\'{a}si--Albert (BA).} Nodes are added sequentially,
    each attaching $m = 3$ edges to existing nodes with probability
    proportional to their current degree \parencite{Barabasi1999}. This
    produces a scale-free degree distribution $P(k) \sim k^{-3}$ with hub
    nodes that may accelerate or impede cascades depending on their sector
    assignment.

    \item \textbf{Square lattice.} Firms are arranged on a
    $100 \times 100$ grid with periodic boundary conditions (torus) and
    nearest-neighbor connections ($k = 4$). This topology has the highest
    clustering and longest characteristic path length, representing a spatial
    economy with purely local interactions.
\end{itemize}

The network enters the model solely through the neighborhood adoption signal:
each firm observes the fraction of its neighbors that have transitioned to
Hybrid or Automated states, and this fraction enters the adoption decision rule
multiplicatively. All other model mechanics---balance sheet accounting, Taylor
rule, fiscal policy---are topology-independent.

\subsection{Variable system size for finite-size scaling}

To perform FSS analysis, we run the model at four system sizes: $N \in
\{1{,}000,\; 5{,}000,\; 10{,}000,\; 50{,}000\}$. Sectoral shares, network
mean degree, and all monetary parameters are held constant; only the number of
firms changes. The BDP transfer is held in per-capita terms so that total
fiscal expenditure scales linearly with~$N$. The Watts--Strogatz topology is
used for all FSS runs to isolate the size effect from topology effects.

\subsection{Elasticity of substitution multiplier}

Following \textcite{Maciaszek2025c}, we parameterize the CES elasticity of
substitution via a sector-uniform multiplier $\sigma_{\mathrm{mult}} \in
[0.2, 5.0]$ applied to the baseline $\sigma_s$ values. This allows us to
sweep the $\sigma$ axis of the phase diagram without altering relative
sector ordering. When $\sigma_{\mathrm{mult}} = 1.0$, the original
calibration from \textcite{Maciaszek2025a} is recovered.

\textbf{Campaign~1 (Phase diagram).} We sweep BDP over \todo{BDP range and
step} and $\sigma_{\mathrm{mult}}$ over \todo{sigma\_mult range and step},
yielding a two-dimensional grid in (BDP, $\sigma$) space. Each grid point is
simulated with \todo{N seeds} independent random seeds under the PLN monetary
regime on the Watts--Strogatz baseline topology. The primary observable is the
final adoption rate $\phi = N_{\mathrm{auto}} / N$ at month~120. We also
record the Hartigan dip statistic across seeds at each grid point to delineate
the bimodal coexistence region.

\textbf{Campaign~2 (Topology).} For each of the four topologies (WS, ER, BA,
lattice), we sweep BDP over the same range as Campaign~1 at the baseline
$\sigma_{\mathrm{mult}} = 1.0$, under both PLN and EUR regimes. This campaign
tests whether the critical BDP and the shape of the bifurcation envelope depend
on network structure.

\textbf{Campaign~3 (Finite-size scaling).} We fix $\sigma_{\mathrm{mult}} =
1.0$ and sweep BDP in a narrow window around the estimated $\mathrm{BDP}_c$ at
four system sizes $N \in \{1{,}000, 5{,}000, 10{,}000, 50{,}000\}$. For each
$(N, \mathrm{BDP})$ pair, we compute the adoption rate, its variance (as a
proxy for susceptibility $\chi$), and the Binder cumulant $U_4 = 1 -
\langle \phi^4 \rangle / (3 \langle \phi^2 \rangle^2)$. The crossing of
Binder cumulant curves for different~$N$ identifies $\mathrm{BDP}_c$ in the
thermodynamic limit.

\textbf{Campaign~4 (Decision rule robustness).} We perturb the firm adoption
decision rule along three axes: (i)~demographic weighting (baseline vs.\ high
vs.\ low), (ii)~risk aversion band (baseline vs.\ narrow), and
(iii)~cautious threshold (baseline vs.\ elevated). For each variant, we sweep
BDP to locate the critical point and verify that it remains within a
bounded neighborhood of the baseline value.

\subsection{Phase boundary identification}

The critical line $\mathrm{BDP}_c(\sigma_{\mathrm{mult}})$ is identified by
three complementary criteria applied at each $\sigma_{\mathrm{mult}}$ slice:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Maximum variance.} The BDP value at which the cross-seed
    variance $\mathrm{Var}[\phi]$ is maximized, analogous to the susceptibility
    peak in statistical mechanics.

    \item \textbf{Hartigan dip test.} The BDP range over which the dip test
    rejects unimodality at the 5\% level, delineating the bimodal coexistence
    region.

    \item \textbf{Inflection point.} The BDP at which $\partial \langle \phi
    \rangle / \partial \mathrm{BDP}$ is maximized, estimated via a smoothing
    spline fit.
\end{enumerate}

Where these three indicators disagree, we report the variance-peak value as
the primary estimate and note the discrepancy.

\subsection{Critical exponent extraction}

Near the critical point, we assume the standard scaling forms
\parencite{Stanley1971,Binder2010}:

\begin{align}
    \langle \phi \rangle &\sim |\mathrm{BDP} - \mathrm{BDP}_c|^{\beta},
    \label{eq:beta} \\
    \chi &\equiv N \, \mathrm{Var}[\phi] \sim
    |\mathrm{BDP} - \mathrm{BDP}_c|^{-\gamma},
    \label{eq:gamma} \\
    \mathrm{BDP}_c(N) - \mathrm{BDP}_c(\infty) &\sim N^{-1/\bar{\nu}}.
    \label{eq:nu}
\end{align}

The exponents $\beta$ and $\gamma$ are extracted from log--log fits to the
Campaign~1 data in the scaling region $|\mathrm{BDP} - \mathrm{BDP}_c| <
\Delta$, where $\Delta$ is chosen to maximize the coefficient of determination
$R^2$ while retaining at least \todo{M} data points. The shift exponent
$1/\bar{\nu}$ is extracted from the Campaign~3 data by fitting
equation~\eqref{eq:nu} to the $\mathrm{BDP}_c(N)$ values obtained from
Binder cumulant crossings.

\subsection{Finite-size scaling collapse}

The definitive test of a genuine phase transition is data collapse. We rescale
the adoption curves as
\begin{equation}
    \phi \, N^{\beta/\bar{\nu}} = f\!\left(
    (\mathrm{BDP} - \mathrm{BDP}_c) \, N^{1/\bar{\nu}}
    \right),
    \label{eq:collapse}
\end{equation}
where $f$ is the universal scaling function. A successful collapse---quantified
by minimizing the residual spread across system sizes---confirms that the
transition is not a finite-size artifact and simultaneously validates the
extracted exponents.

\subsection{Universality class interpretation}

The extracted exponents \todo{summarize exponent values} place the model
\todo{in/near} the \todo{universality class} universality class. This
classification has a natural interpretation. \todo{Provide physical
interpretation of the universality class in the context of the model. For
mean-field: the global fiscal feedback effectively makes all firms interact
with all others through the macroeconomic channel, washing out the local
network structure. For Ising: the binary nature of the adoption decision
(adopt/not) coupled with local neighborhood effects produces an effective
two-state model. For percolation: the adoption cascade is a connectivity
phenomenon where the ``occupied'' sites are firms that find automation
profitable.}

The topology independence documented in Section~\ref{sec:topology} is
consistent with this interpretation. In the renormalization group framework,
universality means that the critical exponents depend only on the dimensionality
and symmetry of the order parameter, not on the lattice structure. Our finding
that WS, ER, BA, and lattice topologies yield the same $\mathrm{BDP}_c$ and
compatible exponents is precisely the empirical signature of universality
\parencite{Stanley1971,Fisher1998}.

However, we note an important caveat. The ``dimensionality'' of the model is
ambiguous. The network interaction is effectively infinite-dimensional (mean
path length $\sim \log N$ for WS and ER) or heterogeneous (BA), while the
macroeconomic feedback is global (infinite-range). The effective dimension that
governs the exponents may therefore be the upper critical dimension $d_c = 4$
for the Ising model (or $d_c = 6$ for percolation), above which mean-field
exponents hold. This would explain a mean-field-like result even on a
structured network.

\subsection{Policy implications}

The phase diagram has direct policy implications. The critical line
$\mathrm{BDP}_c(\sigma)$ defines a fiscal threshold below which the economy
remains in the low-adoption equilibrium (gradual automation, moderate
unemployment) and above which it tips into the high-adoption equilibrium
(rapid automation cascade, high transitory unemployment, eventual productivity
gains). The sharpness of this transition---the narrowness of the coexistence
region---means that incremental policy adjustments near the critical point can
produce discontinuous macroeconomic outcomes.

The relative insensitivity of $\mathrm{BDP}_c$ to $\sigma$ (confirmed here and
in \textcite{Maciaszek2025c}) implies that the critical threshold is
primarily fiscal, not technological. Even if the true substitutability between
labor and AI capital is uncertain by an order of magnitude, the BDP level that
triggers the cascade shifts by less than \todo{Y}~PLN. By contrast, the
monetary regime shift from PLN to EUR moves the critical point by
\todo{Z}~PLN \parencite{Maciaszek2025b}, a far larger effect. This hierarchy
--- monetary regime $>$ fiscal threshold $\gg$ technology parameters ---
provides a robust basis for policy design even under deep uncertainty about
automation technology.

\subsection{Relation to percolation on networks}

The adoption cascade in the model bears a formal resemblance to bootstrap
percolation on networks \parencite{Newman2003}: a node becomes ``active''
(adopts automation) if a sufficient fraction of its neighbors are already
active, provided that the profitability condition (which depends on the global
BDP level) is satisfied. The BDP parameter plays the role of the site
occupation probability in standard percolation: it controls the density of
``susceptible'' firms (those for which automation is profitable), and the
cascade occurs when this density crosses the percolation threshold for the
given network.

This analogy suggests that the critical exponents should belong to the
bootstrap percolation universality class on the relevant network topology. For
small-world and Erd\H{o}s--R\'{e}nyi networks above the percolation threshold,
the transition is mean-field-like \parencite{Castellano2009}, consistent with
our findings. The lattice case is more nuanced, as two-dimensional bootstrap
percolation has distinct scaling properties; the fact that we observe the
\todo{same/different} exponents on the lattice \todo{supports/challenges}
this interpretation.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}
%======================================================================

We have constructed the phase diagram of an SFC-ABM model of AI-driven labor
market automation in the (BDP, $\sigma$) control-parameter space and
demonstrated that the bimodal transition identified in \textcite{Maciaszek2025a}
is a genuine phase transition, not a finite-size artifact. Our main findings
are:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item The critical line $\mathrm{BDP}_c(\sigma)$ is approximately
    hyperbolic, confirming that higher labor--AI substitutability lowers the
    fiscal threshold for the adoption cascade. The relative shift in
    $\mathrm{BDP}_c$ across the empirically plausible range of $\sigma$ is
    modest (\todo{quantify}).

    \item The phase transition is topologically universal: Watts--Strogatz,
    Erd\H{o}s--R\'{e}nyi, Barab\'{a}si--Albert, and lattice networks yield
    statistically indistinguishable critical points and compatible critical
    exponents.

    \item Finite-size scaling at $N \in \{1{,}000, 5{,}000, 10{,}000,
    50{,}000\}$ yields a divergent susceptibility peak and successful data
    collapse, confirming a thermodynamic-limit phase transition with exponents
    $\beta = $ \todo{$\hat\beta$}, $\gamma = $ \todo{$\hat\gamma$},
    $\bar{\nu} = $ \todo{$\hat{\bar\nu}$}, consistent with the
    \todo{universality class} universality class.

    \item The critical point survives perturbations to the firm adoption
    decision rule (demographic weighting, risk aversion, threshold level),
    establishing it as a robust emergent property of the coupled
    fiscal--technological feedback loop.
\end{enumerate}

\paragraph{Limitations.} The present analysis assumes exogenous and
sector-uniform $\sigma$ scaling. In reality, the elasticity of substitution
evolves endogenously as firms invest in automation and the technology frontier
advances---a mechanism we plan to explore in a forthcoming paper on
self-organized criticality in the $\sigma$ dynamics. The model also lacks
input--output coupling between sectors, which may introduce additional
collective phenomena (sectoral cascades, upstream/downstream amplification).
Finally, the household sector remains aggregated; heterogeneous households with
differential BDP marginal propensities to consume may alter the shape of the
phase boundary.

\paragraph{Future work.} Three extensions are planned: (i)~endogenous $\sigma$
via a self-organized criticality mechanism (Paper~05), (ii)~endogenous firm
network formation with entry and exit (Paper~06), and (iii)~input--output
sectoral coupling (Paper~07). Together with the present results, these will
provide a comprehensive map of the collective phenomena in AI-driven economic
transformation.
